## Decisions by the user (2026-11-04)
1. **Viewport:** keeps the picture's own ratio, 280:192 = 1.458:1 (not 4:3). It is never stretched; black bars take the rest, horizontally and vertically, for 4:3, 16:9, 16:10, 21:9 and 32:9. Scaling is nearest neighbour. (My reading, to be corrected if wrong: the viewport is the largest WHOLE-NUMBER multiple of 280x192 that fits, so every Apple pixel is an exact block; below 1x it is shrunk by the largest fraction that fits.)
2. **Esc:** the original Esc behaviour (pause until any key) is replaced by the pause menu (Section 3).
3. **Renderer:** Direct3D 11 first ("Section 0"), because CRT filters, HDR, VSync and frame pacing need it.

## Audit of the existing program (before Section 0)
* Rendering: GDI `StretchDIBits` at a whole-number scale, repaint from a coarse window timer. Window: fixed client size 280x192 x scale; F11/Alt+Enter borderless full screen; Ctrl+Plus/Minus scale; F9 mute.
* Timing: the worker thread runs the game from the virtual 1,022,727 Hz cycle clock; the window thread only shows the newest page under a lock. So window changes cannot change game speed. One game tick is about 2,927 cycles = about 349 ticks per second, much faster than any display refresh (matters for Section 11).
* Input: window thread turns key events into Apple II key codes in a queue. Settings: none are saved yet. Menus: only the game's own text screens. Audio: WASAPI mixer. Build: build.bat (cl) + CMakeLists.txt, no third-party libraries.

## Decision 4 (2026-11-04, for Section 4): Fullscreen vs Borderless Fullscreen
User: "Go with your recommendation." So: **Fullscreen = true exclusive fullscreen** that switches the display to the chosen Resolution (Section 5), with a safe fallback when the mode is unsupported; **Borderless Fullscreen = the current borderless window at the desktop's native resolution, and the default**; the settings page notes that Borderless gives the sharpest picture because exclusive mode's scaling is done by the monitor/GPU. Reasons: on Windows 10/11 a flip-model borderless window has the same latency as exclusive, alt-tab and overlays are better, and VRR/HDR work from borderless; exclusive only adds a real resolution change, a forced refresh rate and old-hardware compatibility.

## Section 7 - Graphics Settings: Background (first part, 2026-11-04, TASK-034-7)
**Done: Black and Ambient Glow, the setting, the menu entry, the settings key.** **Open: Apple II CRT Monitor Bezel and Apple II Desktop**: the asset research is in docs/ASSET_RESEARCH.md (nothing downloaded); the decision (draw them in code / a specific photo / your own pictures) is the user's. The two options will be added to the same setting when decided.
* **Setting:** pause menu > Graphics Settings > "Background: Black / Ambient Glow" (Enter, Right = next, Left = previous, click); settings file key `background=black|glow`; written at once, restored at the next start.
* **Black:** the bars are exactly black (V).
* **Ambient Glow (Direct3D only; with the GDI fallback the bars stay black):** the game picture is reduced to a 14 x 10 grid of average colours (`glow.h`); the shader gives the bars the colour of the nearest point of the picture's edge (smoothly interpolated between the cells), at most 25 % of the cell's colour right at the edge, fading with the distance (about a sixth of the picture's size); 1/255 of noise hides the steps. The hue is the picture's; brightness is lifted so a cell with a little content shows its colour (a patch of about 8 white pixels in a cell is enough), empty cells stay black. The cells change slowly (time constant 0.25 s) so that the blinking of the game (energizers, 1UP, ghosts) does not flicker the background; a picture flashing on and off every frame gives a perfectly steady glow (V). The glow is computed from the game's own picture, not from the dimmed picture of the pause menu, so opening the menu does not change the bars (V).

## Section 8 - Graphics Settings: Colour Mode (done 2026-11-04, TASK-034-8)

The mode is named as in the specification; by the user's decision (research document, section 7) the monochrome look is Apple's own **green** monitor (P31-like phosphor), not white.
* **Native Black & White is made from the DOTS, not by turning the colour picture grey** (`src/video/monochrome.{h,cpp}`, pure): the picture is **560 dot positions across**: a hi-res dot is two positions wide and a byte with the palette bit (bit 7) is drawn **one position further to the right** (the half-dot shift); text dots are two positions wide; a lo-res colour is the 4-position pattern of its four bits (so each colour is a grey level or pattern: black 0 of 14 lit, white 14 of 14, colours 5 and 10 about half); anything else: a dot with any light is lit. Then the phosphor: peak colour (0.20, 1.00, 0.38) of full green, black exactly black. |---|---|---|
| Scanlines | Off / Low / Medium / High (gap darkness 25 / 45 / 70 %) | dark gap between picture lines, drawn per screen pixel from the exact line position; at scale 2 a one-pixel gap, at scale 1 nothing (no room) |

## Section 11 - Graphics Settings: FPS (done 2026-11-04, TASK-034-11)
Pause menu > Graphics Settings > "FPS: Native / 30 FPS / 60 FPS / 120 FPS / 144 FPS / Unlocked" (Enter, Right = next, Left = previous, click); settings key `fps=native|30|60|120|144|unlocked`; written at once, restored at the next start; default Native. ### The timing architecture, as investigated before anything was changed
* **The game** runs on its own worker thread from the virtual 1,022,727 Hz cycle clock (`CycleClock::cyclesDue`): each pass of its loop asks how many cycles real time has earned, runs the ticks that fit (the budget minus each tick's modelled cost), publishes the 8 KB picture page, sleeps. The speed comes from the clock, never from how often the loop wakes, and the window and the game share nothing but the newest page (under a lock). **Nothing about the simulation had to change, and nothing did.**
* **Measured in the real program (12 s runs, tools/test_fps.ps1):** while a level is being played the game runs **about 209 ticks a second** (817 ticks in 3.9 s; the ticks cost 2,927 cycles at the cheapest and more when Pac-Man eats or sound plays; the figure of 349 a second I had quoted earlier is the cheapest case, not the average).
* **What was wrong with how the pictures reached the screen (found by measurement, not guessed):** (1) `sleep_for(2 ms)` really sleeps **15.6 ms** on this Windows (probe: mean 15.57 ms, even after `timeBeginPeriod(1)`), so the worker woke 64 times a second and published its pictures in bursts of about five ticks; (2) the window repainted from a `WM_TIMER`, also about every 15.6 ms; (3) the afterglow and glow used `GetTickCount64`, also 15.6 ms steps; (4) the ambient-glow backdrop (a blur of the whole picture) was worked out for every picture even when another background was chosen, costing 2.5 ms of the 2.8 ms a picture took. Together: about 64 irregular pictures a second whatever the monitor could do.
* **What was changed (display side only):** the worker sleeps with a high-resolution waitable timer (`precisesleep.h`, wakes within about 0.3 ms: measured 2.3 ms for a 2 ms request) and publishes the picture **after every tick**; the window runs its own paced message loop (`GameWindow::run`): pictures are shown on a fixed grid of times (next = previous + period, so a late wake-up does not shift it; after a long stall the grid restarts), not from the window timer (that stays only for dragging/sizing the window, when Windows runs its own loop); times are taken from the performance counter; the ambient glow is only worked out while it is shown (a picture now takes 0.3 ms instead of 2.8 ms).
### The options
* **Native:** one picture per Apple II video frame, 262 lines x 65 cycles = **17,030 cycles = 16.65 ms = 60.05 pictures a second** on the game's cycle clock (the real machine's stretched 65th cycle would give 59.92; not modelled, as in the cycle clock itself, U). The grid is not locked to the game's ticks, just as the real machine's video was not.
* **30 / 60 / 120 / 144:** exactly that many pictures a second; every grid time shows the newest picture (the same one again if the game has nothing new, which changes nothing visible and keeps the rate honest for overlays and VRR).
* **Unlocked:** a picture as soon as the game has a new one (about 209 a second in play; the window can draw about 1,000 a second: measured 0.31 ms per picture), no cap. Pictures with nothing new are not repeated (they would change nothing and only heat the graphics card): say so if you want literal repeating instead.
* **Interpolation: not needed, not built, and the reason is a measurement:** every picture shown is a real state of the game, no blending, no guessing. The game produces about 209 distinct states a second and each tick is published; a 144 Hz frame therefore shows a state at most one tick (about 5 ms of motion) away from the exact time, which cannot be seen; 30 / 60 / 120 / 144 never wait for a state that does not exist. Interpolating between ticks would mean redrawing the sprites at positions the game never had (or cross-fading two pictures, which smears Pac-Man and the ghosts), i.e. changing what the original looked like; that needs your approval, so it is a proposal, not part of this section: **"Smooth Motion"** (an option, off by default) could draw Pac-Man and the ghosts at sub-tick positions from the game state, only if you want it, with the cost that the sprite drawing then differs from the original's.
* **Never changes the game's speed (checked in the real program):** tools/test_fps.ps1 runs the real game for 12 s at each setting: ticks a second **209.1 .. 209.3** in all six (within 0.1 %), pictures a second **60.1 / 30.1 / 60.1 / 120.0 / 144.0 / 208.1** (Native, 30, 60, 120, 144, Unlocked), steady to within 0.3-3 ms.
* **Checked (V), unit tests:** the words, the settings round trip, the Native period (16.65 ms from 17,030 cycles); the precise sleep wakes in under 4 ms (ordinary: 15.6); the paced loop at each rate against a feeder publishing a new picture every millisecond: 60.7 / 30.0 / 60.0 / 120.7 / 143.3 pictures a second (the first 0.3 s excluded), deviation 0.2 ms, Unlocked 1,078 a second; the menu keys, mouse, settings, restore, and the entry's text; the full suite 234 / 0 and the real-program checks (9 + 27 + 4 + the FPS script).
* **Not verified (U):** the pictures actually reaching the glass at those rates (the Windows compositor and the monitor decide what is shown: Present still uses sync interval 0, which Section 12 replaces with the VSync setting); behaviour of the 120 / 144 settings on a monitor that cannot show them (the window still produces them; the monitor shows what it can); high-resolution timer on Windows older than 10 version 1803 (falls back to the ordinary sleep: the pictures are then paced less precisely).

### Section 11, finding after the user's test (2026-11-04): why the FPS options do not feel different, and the slowdown
Measured in the real program (--log now also records these; scratch/play_probe.ps1 plays with scripted arrow keys): the game runs about 190-210 ticks a second, but **only about 1 tick in 3 changes the picture** (777 of 2,277 ticks in an idle level; 1,605 of 4,234 ticks in 32 s of play): **about 50-65 different pictures a second**. So Native / 60 already show every change the original ever produced; 120 / 144 / Unlocked can only repeat pictures. The earlier statement in this section that interpolation was not needed counted ticks, not picture changes, and was wrong in effect.
The slowdown is the original's: the tick cost (modelled from the drawing work of the tick, as the original's 6502 spent it) is **2,987 to 24,463 cycles, mean 4,852**; about 10 % of the ticks (421 of 4,234) cost more than twice the typical tick, i.e. the game's speed dips from about 340 to about 40 ticks a second at those moments. Proposals (nothing built, waiting for the user's decision): (1) "Gameplay Speed: Original / Steady" (every tick takes the same time, the mean, so the average pace is kept; the order and content of the ticks do not change); (2) "Smooth Motion" (sprite-aware interpolation of Pac-Man and the ghosts between ticks, from the game's own draw calls, never blending pictures).

### Section 11, correction after the user's test (2026-11-04): the root cause of the slow motion at a power pill, and the pacing of the modern modes (supersedes "Gameplay Speed", "Steady" and "Steady Fast" above)
**The user's report:** Original: stutters and feels slower when a power pill is eaten; Steady: the whole game drastically slower after a power pill; Steady Fast: the whole game too fast. The user asked to find the cause instead of turning speed numbers, to remove Steady Fast, and to make Steady not a separate setting but the baseline timing of all modern FPS modes (30 / 60 / 120 / 144 / Unlocked), with Native = the original's timing unchanged.
**Why the previous implementations were wrong (found in the code and confirmed in the real program):**
1. The original's pace is not "one tick = a fixed time". A tick lasts as long as the drawing it does (2,987 cycles idle, about 7,000 moving, about 16,000 when a dot is eaten), **and the original's logic is tuned to that.** Pac-Man's step countdown is reloaded with `$7851[level]` (9 ticks at level 1) **plus 1 for each frightened ghost among ghosts 2 and 4 = 11 ticks while the ghosts are blue** (docs/PACMAN_RULES.md, steptimer.cpp: 797 of 797 reloads matched the oracle). Blue ghosts are drawn less, so the ticks of that phase are about 17 % shorter (4,100 against 4,900 cycles): the longer countdown and the shorter ticks nearly cancel (11 x 4,100 = 45.1k against 9 x 4,900 = 44.1k cycles: +2 %). Measured in the real program: Pac-Man's steps a second, before the pill 18.3, after 17.6 (96 %).
2. **A fixed tick time (my first Steady, 4,870 cycles) removed the shorter ticks but kept the longer countdown**: 11 x 4,870 = 53.6k against 9 x 4,870 = 43.8k: Pac-Man **22 % slower** the moment a power pill was eaten, and the ghosts' half-speed (their delay table 10 -> 19 ticks, also tuned to the shorter ticks) not offset either: "drastically slower". That is the bug. **Steady Fast was the wrong cure** (a bigger constant: faster everywhere, still unbalanced) and is removed. No single constant can be right: the right tick time depends on the situation, and the original supplies it.
3. The original's stutter is the same cause seen from the other side: its ticks are uneven (a dot's tick costs 16 ms) while its logic expects them to average out.
**What the modern modes do now (`src/app/pacing.h`):** the tick time is the **running average of the cost the original's ticks have** (exponential average over about 128 ticks, 0.6 s). It keeps the original's pace in every situation and follows its changes (at a power pill within 0.7 s, measured), and takes the hitches out: a dot's 16 ms no longer stops the game. Nothing is multiplied or tuned. The game's logic never sees the tick time. The long sequences (death, level end, intro) are not averaged. The sound stand-stills of the energizer, fruit and bonus-life sounds (9, 88, 96 ms) are dropped in the modern modes (the sounds still play whole at the event); the ghost-eaten stand-still stays in all modes.
**Settings:** no "Gameplay Speed" row, no `gameplay_speed` key any more (an old file's key is ignored). **Native** = the original's timing exactly; **30, 60, 120, 144, Unlocked** = the smoothed pace. The FPS setting only chooses how often pictures are shown (Unlocked: no cap, a picture for every new state).
**Measured (real program, tools/test_pacing.ps1, a power pill eaten about 4 s in):** Native: ticks a second 209 -> 229, Pac-Man steps 18.3 -> 17.6, ordinary ticks 2,987 .. 24,463 cycles; 60 FPS: 207 -> 224, 18.0 -> 17.4, ordinary ticks 4,053 .. 5,562 cycles; the pace before the pill 209 against 207 ticks a second (the same average); Pac-Man and the ghosts keep the original's pace through the pill in both. Unit tests: Native = the old timing for every sound and tick; the smoothed pace has the same mean and moves by under 5 % of itself on a stretch with 3,000 to 16,200-cycle ticks; it follows a change of situation within 174 ticks; the long sequences are not touched; a fixed tick would make Pac-Man 21 % slower after the pill (the arithmetic above as a test). Suite 236 / 0; the other real-program scripts unchanged.
**What the player will still see at a power pill, because it is the game's own rule and not timing:** the blue ghosts move at about half speed (their step delay table says 10 for normal and 19 for frightened at level 1; measured 79 -> 56 moves a second). Pac-Man's pace is kept. If that half-speed of the ghosts is what looks like "slow motion", it is the original's design (verified against the oracle); changing it would change the game and needs the user's approval.
**Not verified (U):** how this feels to play compared with the user's memory of the original: the pace is the original's by measurement; the smoothing window (128 ticks) is a judgement (a shorter one follows the situation faster and smooths less).

### Section 11, second correction (user report, 2026-11-05): Pac-Man's pace at a power pill, and FPS settings that look the same
**The user's report:** (1) at a power pill Pac-Man himself slows down, and the pellets are eaten at a slower rate (heard); every mode but Native must keep Pac-Man's movement, the pellets and the pace constant; (2) the FPS setting makes no visible difference: higher settings must be visibly smoother while the simulation keeps exactly the same speed. Both to be investigated before changing anything, and not to be called done on measurements alone.
**1. Gameplay pace: what the investigation found**
* A clean experiment (headless, virtual time = the summed tick costs, the real game core, no sprites involved; the ghosts' blue flags set by hand, Pac-Man walking by himself): **in the original's own timing, with no dots to eat, Pac-Man's steps a second go from 24.0 to 23.0 when the ghosts turn blue (-4 %); with dots, steps 19.6 -> 20.0 (+2 %) and pellets a second 6.1 -> 5.7 (-7 %).** So the original itself loses a few per cent of Pac-Man's pace and of the pellets (its own compensation, the shorter ticks of the blue phase, is not exact), and my earlier modern pacing, which only followed the original's average tick cost, inherited that. The -7 % of the pellets is what the user hears. (The ghosts' half speed is the game's rule and stays.)
* **The fix (`src/app/pacing.h`, modern modes only):** the tick time is now (the original's average tick cost in calm play) x (the calm countdown + the ticks eating adds) / (the countdown now + the ticks eating adds), so **Pac-Man's whole step takes the same real time whatever the ghosts do**. The ticks that eating adds to his steps (the delay of the item, one more tick on the reloads after it: StepTimer::itemEaten, F3) are not modelled but **learned from his own steps** (an average over about 8 steps; `SmoothPacer::act`). `StepTimer::reloadValue` / `calmReloadValue` expose the countdown. Native is untouched.
* **Measured after the fix (same experiment): no eating: calm 23.9 -> blue 24.2 steps a second (101 %, the original: 96 %); with eating: steps 19.9 -> 20.8 and pellets 6.2 -> 6.0 (96 %; the original 93 %).** The eating case is a random walk whose share of eating steps differs between the two phases by itself (a few per cent either way), so it is held to 7 % / 10 % in the test, the no-eating case to 2 %. Real program (tools/test_pacing.ps1, a power pill eaten by the test aid): Pac-Man's step timer fires 21.5 -> 21.4 a second in Native, 21.5 -> 21.6 at 60 FPS.
* Not verified by me, and said plainly: how it feels to play. If Pac-Man or the pellets still feel slower to the user at a power pill, the next step is to record the user's own run (the log has a line a second) and compare.
**2. Rendering smoothness: why the FPS setting made no visible difference**
* **Cause (measured):** the game changes the picture only 50 to 90 times a second (Pac-Man about 20 steps a second, a ghost 10 to 50), and every picture the original ever makes was already being shown at 60 FPS; a higher FPS could only show the same picture again. Pacing the pictures better (Section 11, first part) could not change that. The real program confirms it: at 144 FPS the pictures that DIFFER from the one before were 59 a second, the same as Native's 50.
* **The fix: smooth motion in every mode but Native** (`src/app/motion.h`, the window's snapshot history, the renderer's sprite pass). Pac-Man and the ghosts glide in a straight line from the place of one of their steps to the place of the next, over the time between the two, at the resolution of the window (fractions of a game pixel). Nothing is predicted or invented: every place shown lies on the line between two places the game really had, and **at the time of a step the picture is exactly the original's** (tested pixel for pixel, in colour and in Native Black & White). The game's state, its timing and its pictures are untouched.
* **How it is built:** the game core's draw calls now say whose they are (`DrawCall::actor`); the driver keeps a **background page** (the page without the images of the actors: the same calls without the actors' image draws), the list of where the actors moved to in each tick, and whether the tick was an ordinary one (`GameDriver::backgroundPage / lastMoves / interpolatable`; checked over 4,000 real ticks: the page and the background differ only inside the rectangle around the actors, 0 pixels outside); the game thread publishes every tick as a picture with its time (`GameWindow::setFrame`; its own smooth clock). The window keeps the last second of them, follows each actor's steps (`ActorTrack`), picks the background of the time asked and the sprite of each actor (the pixels that differ from the background around the place of its step: contact colours with walls and dots included), and the renderer draws the background and the sprites into a picture of the window's size, then the CRT filter works on that (the filter's shader reads a source of any size now). **Cost: the window shows the game about 60 to 90 ms behind (the length of the step the actor is gliding along, the delay that makes the glide known); this is the price of any interpolation.**
* **Where it is not used (the picture is shown as it is):** Native, the pause menu, the text and lo-res screens, the sequences (death, READY), the afterglow of the CRT filter (it works on whole pictures; setting it turns the smooth motion off), the software GDI fallback. A sprite that touches another actor's rectangle takes a few of its pixels along (rare). The four energizers blink in both, shifted by the delay (checked: the same on-off pattern, 5 shots on, 5 off).
* **Unlocked** now really is uncapped: the swap chain allows tearing where the system does (the present no longer waits for the compositor: measured 2.7 ms -> 0.08 ms a present) and the loop does not wait between pictures: **about 7,000 pictures a second on the development PC**, each with the actors at a new place; the game's speed is unchanged. (Tearing may show with Unlocked, as the specification allows; Section 12 will give VSync its own setting.)
* **Measured in the real program (tools/test_fps.ps1; pictures that differ from the one before, per second of play):** Native 50; **30 FPS 31; 60 FPS 60; 120 FPS 120; 144 FPS 143; Unlocked 1,866**; the game's speed 208.7 to 209.5 ticks a second in all of them. Unit tests: the glide (halfway at half the time, stands still, no crawl after a long stand, no glide across a jump); the renderer's sprites at fractions of a pixel (0 wrong pixels of 860,160); the window: 64 pictures over 8 steps of a moving block show 65 different places, never backwards, in colour and in monochrome; Native shows none of that; and at the time of a step the picture equals the original's.
* **How a check of mine failed, and what caught it:** the first live run showed 0 frames with the actors gliding although all unit tests passed: the game loop still published a plain picture at the end of every batch, which switched the smooth motion off; found only because the log counts the gliding frames (`smooth_frames`), fixed, re-measured. The first "different pictures" figure counted page versions and so could not tell smooth from not smooth; it now compares picture content.
