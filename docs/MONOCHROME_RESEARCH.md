## 1. What the Apple II sent to a monochrome monitor
* **The video signal has no colour.** The Apple II's video circuit outputs one black-and-white signal; the colours of the hi-res screen on a colour TV are an artifact: the TV's colour decoder reads the timing of the dots. A monochrome monitor therefore shows every lit dot in the one colour of its phosphor, and no other shade comes from the Apple. (Wikipedia, Apple II graphics; the blog and technote results agree.)
* **The half-dot shift is real on a monochrome screen.** In hi-res the high bit of each byte (the "palette bit", which the game sets on most of its bytes) shifts the dots of that byte right by half a dot. On a colour screen that changes the colour; on a monochrome screen it shows as a dotted line that steps sideways by half a pixel. So an authentic monochrome picture is **560 dots wide**, not 280: a byte with the palette bit set draws its dots one 560-dot position further right. (Wikipedia; Apple IIe Technical Note #3; izapple2 source.)
* **Neighbouring dots.** On colour screens two horizontally adjacent lit dots make white and a lone dot makes a colour; on a monochrome monitor the pairs "can be discerned" as they are: a lone dot is a lone dot, two dots a short bar. Single-dot-wide patterns (the game draws its maze walls as **every second dot**, because on a colour screen that looks like a solid blue line) show as **dotted lines** on a monochrome monitor. (Wikipedia talk page; our own docs/GRAPHICS.md section 3: flag `$1D`, step 2.) This is the main visible difference: the maze loses its solid blue walls.
* **Lo-res (the title screens) and text.** Lo-res colour blocks are made the same way (a 4-bit pattern per colour repeated along the line), so on a monochrome monitor each colour is a different **pattern or grey level** (how many of its four bits are set). Text is plain phosphor-coloured characters, inverse = dark on lit, flash = alternating. (paleotronic.com and izapple2 results; not verified for exact bit order.)
* **Bandwidth.** The Apple Monitor II / III video amplifiers were wide enough to show single dots (a Monitor II bandwidth of about 18 MHz against about 15 MHz for the Monitor III was reported by a forum post: not verified). An alternating dot pattern is 3.6 MHz, so the dotted walls stay dotted; the picture is slightly soft, never as hard-edged as a pixel grid. (My inference from the figures, not a measurement.)

## 2. The monochrome monitors of the period
* **Apple's own were green.** The Apple Monitor II and III used a green phosphor, listed in Apple's specification as **EIA type P31**, with a high-contrast tube and "an extremely dark background" (Apple advertisement quoted in the results). They were built for Apple by other makers (sources disagree: Sanyo, Samsung, Hitachi).
* **Phosphor types in general** (Wikipedia, Monochrome monitor): P1 green (the earliest green screens), **P31 green** (brighter, short persistence, the 1980s standard green), **P3 amber**, **P4 white** (black-and-white TVs and some monitors). Amber and white versions existed from other makers for the Apple II; I found nothing specific about the Zenith ZVM-122 or the Amdek 300 series (not verified).
* **What emulators do (reference, not to be copied):** AppleWin has "Monochrome" modes with a choice of colour: fixed Amber, Green and White presets and a custom colour (its green preset is reported as RGB 0x00,0xC0,0x00) and, in newer versions, "half-pixel" modes that implement the half-dot shift (source: AppleWin help page and Video.cpp on git.applefritter.com). AppleWin is GPL-licensed: its code and tables must NOT be copied into this project; only the idea (modes, half-dot shift) is used, and I would derive the colours myself.

## 3. What this project has now
`video::renderArgb` (colour.cpp) classifies each lit dot at 280-dot resolution into the hi-res colours (lone dot: violet/green or blue/orange by position and palette bit; a dot with a lit neighbour: white). It does not model the half-dot shift (the colour rules replace it) and it has no monochrome output. For the **text and lo-res pages** (boot menus, prompts, attract screens) the port makes its own ARGB pictures from the Apple II character ROM and the lo-res memory.

## 4. Proposal for "Native Black & White" (nothing built yet)
1. **Source of truth: the dots, not the colours.** Render the monochrome picture from the dot bits themselves: a 560 x 192 picture where every dot is two positions wide and a byte with the palette bit set is one position further right (the half-dot shift). No grey-scale conversion of the colour picture.
2. **One phosphor colour for everything lit**, black for everything else; the phosphor decides the look: white (a black-and-white monitor or TV, matches the name "Black & White"), **green P31** (Apple's own Monitor II/III: the most "Apple" look), or amber. Slight softness along the line (the monitor's limited bandwidth) so single dots are not razor sharp, and a very dark background like the real tubes; strength modest and fixed (no choice of blur) so it stays compatible with the CRT filter (Section 9) and HDR (Section 10).
3. **Distinguishable game elements:** all lit dots are the same colour, so the game's elements are told apart by shape, by the dotted maze walls against solid sprites, and by brightness where a pattern is partial (lo-res greys). I would check on real frames that Pac-Man, ghosts, dots and energizers remain recognisable and add a test that compares the monochrome picture's structure with the colour picture's (the same lit dots, no dots added or lost).
4. **Text and lo-res pages:** text in the phosphor colour; lo-res colours as the patterns or grey levels their four bits give.
5. **Works with everything else:** only the game picture is recoloured; backgrounds, the pause menu overlay (also drawn in the phosphor colour in this mode), the bars and the glow follow the same phosphor colour for the glow only. Settings key `colour_mode=original|mono`; menu entry in Graphics Settings.
6. **Tests:** every lit dot of the original-colour picture is lit in the mono picture and vice versa (no dots lost, none added); the half-dot shift (a palette-bit byte's dots are exactly one position right); lo-res grey levels; text; the picture is byte-equal when Original Colour is chosen (nothing changes by default); persistence; game and clock untouched.

## 5. Decisions needed from you
1. **Phosphor colour for "Native Black & White":** (a) **white** (matches the name; a black-and-white monitor/TV, P4), (b) **green** (Apple's own monochrome monitors, P31), or (c) white by default now, with a green/amber choice added later as a modernization option. My recommendation: (c).
2. **Half-dot shift:** build the 560-dot picture so palette-bit bytes sit half a dot to the right (authentic: the "half-pixel dance" on the game's sprites and walls), or keep a 280-dot picture and ignore the shift (simpler, crisper, not authentic). My recommendation: build the 560-dot picture; at the odd whole-number scales the half-dot columns are 1 pixel wider or narrower, which is how a real monitor's dots look.
3. **Softness:** a slight horizontal softness (the monitor's bandwidth) or hard dots. My recommendation: slight softness, fixed, subtle.
4. **Assets:** none are needed; the monochrome picture is computed. (The only outside things used are facts from the references above; no files, no code.)
